\begin{abstract}
Transient-grating spectroscopy probes carrier relaxation and spatial diffusion through the decay of a photoinduced grating. This report analyzes the supplied GaAs autocorrelation, spectrum, pump--probe and transient-grating traces. Fits with nonlinear fringe calibration yield selected Gaussian pulse-intensity FWHM estimates of $(112.89\pm0.10)\,\si{fs}$ before and $(93.902\pm0.054)\,\si{fs}$ after the compressor, above the $(83.87\pm0.24)\,\si{fs}$ Gaussian transform-limit estimate. These uncertainties are propagated formal one-standard-error values, excluding calibration and model uncertainty; fit-window variation is larger. The diffusivity uncertainty below is the regression slope standard error. Biexponential fits summarize the transient traces over the selected interval, while the slow finite-wave-vector rates give an exploratory ambipolar diffusivity of $(3.71\pm0.70)\times10^{-3}\,\si{\metre\squared\per\second}$. The pump--probe rate is inconsistent with the transient-grating extrapolation, limiting the physical interpretation of that estimate. The data do not directly measure hot-carrier temperature or electron--phonon coupling.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Ultrafast optical pulses create and probe non-equilibrium carriers in semiconductors. Their temporal relaxation and spatial transport can be studied by measuring the change in reflected or diffracted probe light after excitation. In transient-grating (TG) spectroscopy, two pump beams interfere in the sample and create a periodic carrier-density pattern. Diffusion reduces the grating amplitude, and the decay rate depends on the grating wave number.

This experiment uses GaAs and combines pulse characterization with pump--probe and TG measurements. The analysis follows the four tasks in the course tutorial: determine pulse duration and spectral bandwidth; assess whether the data can determine hot-carrier cooling and electron--phonon coupling; fit transient-reflectivity and TG decay times; and estimate recombination, ambipolar diffusivity and mobility. The report first derives the relevant models, then describes the optical arrangement and supplied data, and finally presents the results in that order. A final discussion compares the results with their assumptions and available literature.

\section{Theoretical background}
\subsection{Transient-grating formation and carrier decay}\label{sec:decay-theory}
An ultrashort pump pulse creates electron--hole pairs in GaAs. Two pump fields with equal intensity $I_0$ and wave vectors $\mathbf{k}_1,\mathbf{k}_2$ produce the intensity pattern
\begin{align}
I(\mathbf{r})&=|E_1+E_2|^2\\
&=|E_1|^2+|E_2|^2+E_1E_2^*+E_1^*E_2\\
&=2I_0\left[1+\cos\left((\mathbf{k}_1-\mathbf{k}_2)\cdot\mathbf{r}+\phi\right)\right].
\end{align}
For an angle $\theta$ between the beams, $q=|\mathbf{k}_1-\mathbf{k}_2|=2k\sin(\theta/2)$ and
\begin{equation}
\Lambda_{\rm TG}=\frac{2\pi}{q}=\frac{\lambda}{2\sin(\theta/2)}.
\end{equation}
The two-beam interference and resulting spatial carrier modulation are illustrated in Fig.~\ref{fig:tutorial-grating}.

\begin{figure}[!htbp]
\centering\includegraphics[width=.78\linewidth]{figures/tutorial_grating.png}
\caption{Two crossed pump beams create a sinusoidal excitation pattern in the sample. The grating period $\Lambda_{\rm TG}$ sets the spatial wave number $q=2\pi/\Lambda_{\rm TG}$ used in the diffusion analysis. Reproduced from Fig.~4.5 of the course tutorial.}\label{fig:tutorial-grating}
\end{figure}

Write the carrier density as a uniform part plus its sinusoidal grating component,
\begin{equation}
n(x,t)=N_0(t)+N_q(t)\cos(qx).
\end{equation}
Substitution into the tutorial's one-dimensional diffusion--recombination equation gives
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial n(x,t)}{\partial t}=D_a\frac{\partial^2 n(x,t)}{\partial x^2}-\frac{n(x,t)}{\tau_{\rm rec}}.
\end{equation}
Using $\partial_t n=\dot N_0+\dot N_q\cos(qx)$ and $\partial_x^2n=-q^2N_q\cos(qx)$, and equating the coefficients of $1$ and $\cos(qx)$, respectively,
\begin{align}
\dot N_0&=-\frac{N_0}{\tau_{\rm rec}}, & \dot N_q&=-\left(D_aq^2+\frac{1}{\tau_{\rm rec}}\right)N_q.\\
\int_{N_0(0)}^{N_0(t)}\frac{dN_0}{N_0}&=-\int_0^t\frac{dt'}{\tau_{\rm rec}}, &
\int_{N_q(0)}^{N_q(t)}\frac{dN_q}{N_q}&=-\int_0^t\left(D_aq^2+\frac{1}{\tau_{\rm rec}}\right)dt'.
\end{align}
Evaluation yields
\begin{align}
N_0(t)&=N_0(0)e^{-t/\tau_{\rm rec}},\\
N_q(t)&=N_q(0)e^{-(D_aq^2+1/\tau_{\rm rec})t}.
\end{align}
For heterodyne detection with a constant local oscillator and $E_{\rm dif}=\alpha N_q$,
\begin{align}
|E_{\rm LO}+E_{\rm dif}|^2&=|E_{\rm LO}|^2+|\alpha|^2N_q^2
 +2\operatorname{Re}(E_{\rm LO}^*\alpha)N_q,\\
S_{\rm het}&\propto N_q\propto e^{-(D_aq^2+1/\tau_{\rm rec})t}.
\end{align}
Thus the heterodyne decay rate contains $D_aq^2$, with no factor of two from squaring the grating amplitude. Assuming that the reflectivity change is also linear in the corresponding population, the $q=0$ transient-reflectivity and finite-$q$ grating traces therefore have the forms
\begin{align}
 S_{\mathrm{pop}}(t)&=A_{\mathrm{pop}}e^{-t/\tau_{\mathrm{rec}}}+C_{\mathrm{pop}},\\
 S_{\mathrm{TG}}(t)&=A_{\mathrm{TG}}e^{-t/\tau_{\mathrm{TG}}}+C_{\mathrm{TG}},\\
 \frac{1}{\tau_{\mathrm{TG}}}&=\frac{1}{\tau_{\mathrm{rec}}}+D_aq^2.
\end{align}
In $1/\tau_{\rm TG}=1/\tau_{\rm rec}+D_aq^2$, the intercept is $1/\tau_{\rm rec}$ and the slope is $D_a$.

The provided measurement instructions request two decay components to represent faster carrier relaxation and slower processes such as grating diffusion. We therefore use the phenomenological extension
\begin{equation}
S(t,q)=C+A_f e^{-t/\tau_f}+A_s e^{-t/\tau_s},\qquad 0<\tau_f<\tau_s.
\end{equation}
The assignments of the two fitted components are interpretations of their timescales. To estimate diffusion, the slow-component rate $1/\tau_s$ is regressed against $q^2$ for the finite-$q$ TG measurements; the single-population diffusion equation above motivates this comparison, but does not by itself derive the biexponential response.

\subsection{Ambipolar transport}
For ambipolar transport, set the excess electron and hole densities equal, $n=h$, and equate their particle fluxes. With the drift--diffusion expressions
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{j}_e=-D_e\nabla n-\mu_en\mathbf{E},\qquad
\mathbf{j}_h=-D_h\nabla n+\mu_hn\mathbf{E},
\end{equation}
the condition $\mathbf{j}_e=\mathbf{j}_h$ gives
\begin{align}
-(D_e-D_h)\nabla n&=(\mu_e+\mu_h)n\mathbf{E},\\
\mathbf{E}&=\frac{D_h-D_e}{\mu_e+\mu_h}\frac{\nabla n}{n},\\
\mathbf{j}_e&=-\left[D_e+\mu_e\frac{D_h-D_e}{\mu_e+\mu_h}\right]\nabla n\\
&=-\frac{D_e\mu_h+D_h\mu_e}{\mu_e+\mu_h}\nabla n.
\end{align}
Comparing with Fick's law $\mathbf{j}=-D_a\nabla n$ identifies
\begin{align}
D_a&=\frac{D_e\mu_h+D_h\mu_e}{\mu_e+\mu_h},\\
D_i&=\frac{k_BT}{e}\mu_i\quad(i=e,h)\\
\Longrightarrow\quad D_a&=\frac{2\mu_e\mu_h}{\mu_e+\mu_h}\frac{k_BT}{e}=\mu_a\frac{k_BT}{e},\\
\mu_a&=\frac{2\mu_e\mu_h}{\mu_e+\mu_h}.
\end{align}
The Einstein step assumes non-degenerate carriers at a common temperature. At high carrier density, many-body and degeneracy effects can change $D_a$ \cite{young}.

\subsection{Pulse duration and spectral bandwidth}
For a Gaussian pulse intensity $I(t)=I_0e^{-t^2/(2\sigma_t^2)}$, the intensity autocorrelation is
\begin{align}
G(\tau)&=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}I(t)I(t-\tau)\,dt\\
&=I_0^2\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\exp\!\left[-\frac{t^2+(t-\tau)^2}{2\sigma_t^2}\right]dt\\
&=I_0^2e^{-\tau^2/(4\sigma_t^2)}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-(t-\tau/2)^2/\sigma_t^2}\,dt\\
&=I_0^2\sqrt{\pi}\sigma_t e^{-\tau^2/(4\sigma_t^2)}.
\end{align}
Here $t^2+(t-\tau)^2=2(t-\tau/2)^2+\tau^2/2$ and the substitution $u=(t-\tau/2)/\sigma_t$ reduces the integral to $\sigma_t\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-u^2}du=\sqrt{\pi}\sigma_t$. The intensity half-maximum satisfies $e^{-t^2/(2\sigma_t^2)}=1/2$, so $\Delta t_{\rm pulse}=2\sqrt{2\ln2}\,\sigma_t$. The autocorrelation half-maximum satisfies $e^{-\tau^2/(4\sigma_t^2)}=1/2$, so $\Delta t_{\rm AC}=4\sqrt{\ln2}\,\sigma_t=\sqrt{2}\,\Delta t_{\rm pulse}$.

The Gaussian time--bandwidth product follows from the field envelope $a(t)=\sqrt{I_0}e^{-t^2/(4\sigma_t^2)}$. Set $F(\nu)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}a(t)e^{-i2\pi\nu t}\,dt$ and use $a'(t)=-t\,a(t)/(2\sigma_t^2)$. Differentiating and integrating by parts on the real axis gives
\begin{align}
F'(\nu)&=-i2\pi\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}t\,a(t)e^{-i2\pi\nu t}\,dt\\
&=i4\pi\sigma_t^2\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}a'(t)e^{-i2\pi\nu t}\,dt\\
&=i4\pi\sigma_t^2\left([a(t)e^{-i2\pi\nu t}]_{-\infty}^{\infty}+i2\pi\nu F(\nu)\right)\\
&=-8\pi^2\sigma_t^2\nu F(\nu),\\
\int_{F(0)}^{F(\nu)}\frac{dF}{F}&=-8\pi^2\sigma_t^2\int_0^\nu\nu'\,d\nu',\\
F(\nu)&=F(0)e^{-4\pi^2\sigma_t^2\nu^2},\qquad
F(0)=2\sqrt{\pi}\sigma_t\sqrt{I_0},\\
|F(\nu)|^2&=4\pi\sigma_t^2I_0e^{-8\pi^2\sigma_t^2\nu^2}.
\end{align}
The boundary term vanishes because the Gaussian tends to zero at both limits; $F(0)$ follows by substituting $t=2\sigma_t u$ in the Gaussian integral.
At half maximum, $|F(\nu)|^2=|F(0)|^2/2$, so
\begin{align}
8\pi^2\sigma_t^2\nu^2&=\ln2,\\
\Delta\nu&=2|\nu|=\frac{\sqrt{\ln2}}{\sqrt{2}\pi\sigma_t}.
\end{align}
Combining this bandwidth with $\Delta t_{\rm pulse}=2\sqrt{2\ln2}\,\sigma_t$ gives
\begin{align}
\Delta t_{\rm pulse}\Delta\nu
&=2\sqrt{2\ln2}\,\sigma_t\frac{\sqrt{\ln2}}{\sqrt{2}\pi\sigma_t}\\
&=\frac{2\ln2}{\pi}\simeq0.441.
\end{align}
From $\nu=c/\lambda$, differentiation gives $d\nu/d\lambda=-c/\lambda^2$; for a narrow spectrum centered at $\lambda_0$, $\Delta\nu\simeq(c/\lambda_0^2)\Delta\lambda$.

The optical pulse duration can be measured with a second-order autocorrelator based on second-harmonic generation (SHG). The measured intensity is a function of the relative delay between two pulse replicas. For a Gaussian pulse, the intensity autocorrelation FWHM is $\sqrt{2}$ times the pulse-intensity FWHM. The transform-limited Gaussian time-bandwidth product is $\Delta t\Delta\nu\simeq0.441$; a larger product can indicate spectral phase/chirp, or measurement and calibration errors. A spectrometer measures wavelength, with $\Delta\nu\simeq c\Delta\lambda/\lambda_0^2$ for a narrow spectrum. This conversion and the time-bandwidth comparison assume that the fitted spectral profile is representative of the pulse spectrum and approximately Gaussian.

\section{Experimental setup and measurement}
\subsection{Optical arrangement}
The tutorial describes a Kerr-lens mode-locked Ti:sapphire femtosecond laser. Its intensity-dependent refractive index produces self-focusing that favors pulsed operation; a prism compressor compensates dispersion and reduces pulse chirp. For autocorrelation, a Michelson interferometer creates two pulse replicas with adjustable delay and a BBO crystal generates a second-harmonic signal proportional to their temporal overlap. For the sample measurement, the beam is split into pump and probe paths; two pump beams cross on the GaAs sample and make the excitation grating. Two probe beams arranged in BOXCAR geometry provide a diffracted signal and a local oscillator. Their interference gives a heterodyne term proportional to the diffracted field, improving sensitivity over direct intensity detection. The tutorial describes phase modulation of the local oscillator with a rotating coverslip and pump modulation with a chopper; a lock-in amplifier extracts the component at the chopper frequency. The overall optical layout is shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:tutorial-setup}; the individual Michelson autocorrelator and BOXCAR detection principle are shown in Figs.~\ref{fig:tutorial-autocorrelator} and~\ref{fig:tutorial-heterodyne}.

\subsection{Measurement data and calibration}
The supplied data comprise the spectrum file \texttt{spektrum.csv}, three before-compressor traces (\texttt{vor1}--\texttt{vor3}), three after-compressor traces (\texttt{nach1}--\texttt{nach3}), and five transient files: one pump--probe trace and four TG traces. Each transient file contains delay in its first column and repeated scans in the remaining columns. The measurement instructions specify delay in picoseconds and state that the actual sample grating period is one quarter of the phase-mask period encoded in each TG filename; we therefore use $\Lambda_{\rm sample}=\Lambda_{\rm mask}/4$ and $q=2\pi/\Lambda_{\rm sample}$. The exact incidence angles and an independent grating-period calibration were not supplied.

The before/after files are compared in Fig.~\ref{fig:vor-nach}. The \texttt{vor2} record has a fivefold shorter sampling interval and total duration. Sampling interval alone is not a reason for exclusion, since fringe calibration can accommodate it. The relevant problem is the substantial oscillation remaining at the record boundary, quantified in Part I. The other candidates are ranked by residual RMS divided by the raw-signal range in the fitted window. This chooses a representative fit for display; it does not establish which scan has the smallest physical error.

\subsection{Data screening and fit procedure}
The analysis was performed with the Python scripts in \texttt{analysis/}. For each TG dataset with at least eight repeats, the scan-level deviation score is the root-mean-square of each scan's difference from the pointwise median, standardized by the larger of the pointwise robust scale $1.4826\,\mathrm{MAD}$ and its lower-quartile floor. Scores are converted to modified scores using $z_j=0.6745(s_j-\mathrm{median}(s))/\mathrm{MAD}(s)$; scans above 3.5 are flagged. The primary analysis excludes flagged whole scans and documents their deviations against the retained population; no individual delay points are removed. This is a statistical screen, not proof of acquisition failure, so the effect of retaining flagged scans is also calculated. The screening outcome is described in Sec.~\ref{sec:decay-results}; the flagged scans are compared directly with the retained population in Fig.~\ref{fig:excluded-scan-comparison}.

The retained scans are averaged pointwise, with standard errors across repeats. The mean traces are fitted by unweighted least squares for $t\geq\SI{0.5}{ps}$ to the ordered biexponential model in Sec.~\ref{sec:decay-theory}, using each file through its last recorded delay. The standard errors are plotted but are not fit weights; each recorded delay has equal weight, despite nonuniform delay spacing. The PP record extends to \SI{2000}{ps}, whereas the TG records end between \SI{26.6}{ps} and \SI{57}{ps}; the different windows require a sensitivity check. A 300-replicate bootstrap resamples complete retained scans within each dataset, repeats the averaging and fitting, and propagates slow rates through the finite-$q$ regression. The resulting intervals quantify repeat-scan variability conditional on the selected traces and model. They do not include calibration or model uncertainty.

Several experimental conditions needed for full reproducibility were not recorded in the supplied files: measured sample temperature, pump and probe fluence, spot size, sample thickness and orientation, incidence angles, and detector/lock-in settings. The tutorial and \texttt{RT} filenames indicate nominal room-temperature measurements; an actual temperature reading is absent. These missing quantities also limit comparison with literature. Numerical results and sensitivity checks are saved in \texttt{analysis/results.json} and \texttt{analysis/review\_checks.json}.

\subsection{Uncertainty conventions and propagation}\label{sec:uncertainty}
Unless identified as a confidence interval, a quoted $\pm$ value denotes one conditional standard error. Nonlinear-fit errors are obtained from the parameter covariance matrix; regression errors are ordinary least-squares standard errors. Bracketed 95\% intervals for primary transient lifetimes and diffusivity are percentile intervals from resampling complete retained scans. These quantify different sources of variability and are not interchangeable. Calibration, model, trace-selection and temperature uncertainties remain unquantified and are not included in the quoted statistical errors.

For a derived quantity $y=f(\mathbf p)$ with covariance matrix $\Sigma$, first-order propagation uses
\begin{equation}
u_y^2=\nabla f^{\mathsf T}\Sigma\nabla f.
\end{equation}
The full spectral covariance is retained for the peak level $A+B$, half-maximum level $A/2+B$, frequency bandwidth, and transform-limit duration. If $s$ is the fitted pulse width in carrier cycles, then $\Delta t\propto s\lambda_0$ and $\Delta\nu\propto\sigma_\lambda/\lambda_0^2$, so the time--bandwidth product is proportional to $s\sigma_\lambda/\lambda_0$. Its error propagation therefore includes the shared wavelength dependence rather than treating the derived duration and bandwidth as independent. Spectrum and IAC fit parameters from separate records are treated as independent. The common wavelength cancels in the before/after duration ratio. For the nominal intercept lifetime, $u_{1/b}=u_b/b^2$ is a first-order standard error, not a symmetric 95\% interval.

Counts, file identifiers, theoretical constants, analysis thresholds and selected fit-window boundaries are specified inputs, not estimated physical results. Recorded axis coordinates are nominal; their instrument-calibration uncertainties are unavailable. Descriptive statistics such as residual RMS and scan scores are identified separately where their uncertainties have not been estimated. No uncertainty of zero is implied by an unavailable uncertainty. Propagation is reproduced by \texttt{analysis/report\_uncertainties.py}.

\section{Results}
\subsection{Part I: Pulse duration and spectral bandwidth}
The pulse durations follow from Eq.~\eqref{eq:fitted-iac} with the nonlinear fringe calibration derived above. Figure~\ref{fig:autocorrelation} shows the selected before/after traces. We retain the exclusion of \texttt{vor2}: in the last 10\% of its record, the RMS signal relative to its whole-record median is 0.397 recorded units, compared with 0.0081 and 0.0058 for \texttt{vor1} and \texttt{vor3}. Together with Fig.~\ref{fig:vor-nach}, this shows that its right-hand wing remains appreciably oscillatory at the boundary. These edge RMS values are descriptive diagnostics with unquantified uncertainties. The median is a descriptive reference, not an independently measured baseline. A truncated trace could in principle be fitted, but would constrain the baseline and wings less reliably; we use the better-covered repeats instead.

The measured spectrum and Gaussian fit are shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:spectrum}. The nominal wavelength coordinates (calibration uncertainty unavailable) span approximately \SI{345}{nm} to \SI{1041}{nm}. We fitted all recorded points with
\begin{equation}
S(\lambda)=B+A\exp\!\left[-\frac{(\lambda-\lambda_0)^2}{2\sigma_\lambda^2}\right].
\end{equation}
The peak above baseline is $A$ and the fitted peak level is $B+A$. At half peak height above baseline, $S-B=A/2$, so
\begin{align}
e^{-(\lambda-\lambda_0)^2/(2\sigma_\lambda^2)}&=\frac12,\\
|\lambda-\lambda_0|&=\sqrt{2\ln2}\,|\sigma_\lambda|,\\
\Delta\lambda_{\rm FWHM}&=2\sqrt{2\ln2}\,|\sigma_\lambda|.
\end{align}
The fit gives a centre of $(788.656\pm0.013)$ nm, peak intensity of $(13302\pm27)$ a.u., and fitted half-maximum level of $(7902\pm14)$ a.u., with $\Delta\lambda_{\rm FWHM}=(10.916\pm0.032)$ nm. Propagating the full spectral covariance through $\Delta\nu\simeq c\Delta\lambda/\lambda_0^2$ gives $\Delta\nu=(5.261\pm0.015)$ THz and $\Delta t_{\rm TL}=(2\ln2/\pi)/\Delta\nu=(83.87\pm0.24)$ fs. All quoted errors here are formal one-standard-error uncertainties, excluding calibration and model uncertainty. The median measured intensity is approximately \SI{2501}{a.u.}; its measurement uncertainty is unquantified because repeat spectra and an instrument-noise model are unavailable. The median is a descriptive statistic of all recorded wavelengths, not the fitted background $B$.

\subsection{Part II: Hot-carrier cooling and electron--phonon coupling}
Following the email's assignment of the fast component to relaxation of photoexcited carriers, we report a conditional effective cooling-time estimate $\tau_{\rm cool}^{\rm eff}=\tau_f=\SI{0.717}{ps}$, with a 95\% repeat-scan bootstrap confidence interval of $[0.698,0.743]$ ps. Identifying it as a cooling time additionally requires that the fast reflectivity change be proportional to the carrier-temperature excess, with population and optical-response effects separated. That calibration is not supplied. The \SI{0.5}{ps} fit cutoff excludes the earliest thermalization, but does not by itself exclude picosecond cooling: the tutorial distinguishes roughly \SI{100}{fs} carrier thermalization (tutorial estimate; uncertainty not supplied) from electron--lattice equilibration over a few picoseconds \cite{tutorial}.

The present data therefore support a cooling-time estimate under an optical-response model, but do not uniquely determine a carrier-temperature relaxation time. The systematic PP residuals also make the estimate sensitive to the fitted response: adding a slow contribution changes the fast time to $(0.674\pm0.011)$ ps (conditional covariance standard error; Sec.~\ref{sec:chen-model}). Neither that standard error nor the bootstrap interval above includes the uncertainty in assigning an optical decay component to cooling. The quoted effective cooling time is consequently retained as a conditional interpretation of the baseline fit.

To state exactly what a coupling inference would require, assume constant volumetric heat capacities $C_e,C_l$ and an average electron--phonon coefficient $G$ after excitation:
\begin{align}
C_e\dot T_e&=-G(T_e-T_l), & C_l\dot T_l&=G(T_e-T_l),\\
\Delta T&=T_e-T_l, &
\frac{d\Delta T}{dt}&=-G\left(\frac1{C_e}+\frac1{C_l}\right)\Delta T,\\
\int_{\Delta T(0)}^{\Delta T(t)}\frac{d\Delta T'}{\Delta T'}
&=-G\left(\frac1{C_e}+\frac1{C_l}\right)\int_0^t dt',\\
\Delta T(t)&=\Delta T(0)e^{-t/\tau_{\rm cool}}, &
G&=\frac{C_eC_l}{C_e+C_l}\frac1{\tau_{\rm cool}}.
\end{align}
If $C_l\gg C_e$ and $\tau_{\rm cool}=\tau_f$, then $G/C_e\simeq1/\tau_f=\SI{1.394}{\per\pico\second}$, with a conditional bootstrap interval $[1.347,1.432]$ ps$^{-1}$. This is a relaxation rate under explicit assumptions, not a measured volumetric coupling coefficient in W\,m$^{-3}$\,K$^{-1}$. For nondegenerate electrons of fixed density $n$, $C_e=\partial(3nk_BT_e/2)/\partial T_e=3nk_B/2$, but $n$ and the relevant temperature dependence are unknown here. A numerical $G$ is therefore not determined.

The assignment also depends on the fit window: fitting PP only through \SI{33}{ps} gives $\tau_f=(0.579\pm0.010)$ ps, whereas starting the full-record fit at \SI{1}{ps} gives $(0.96\pm0.13)$ ps (conditional fit standard errors). These changes exceed the repeat-scan interval. For context, Hunsche et al. report thermalization within \SI{200}{fs}, while Kash et al. infer an average electron--LO-phonon scattering time near \SI{165}{fs} below $10^{17}$ cm$^{-3}$ \cite{hunsche,kash}. The quoted \SI{200}{fs} is a reported upper bound and \SI{165}{fs} is an approximate literature value; associated uncertainties are not supplied in the cited abstracts. Neither microscopic time is the same observable as the effective temperature-relaxation time or our reflectivity fit component.

\subsection{Part IV: Recombination, ambipolar diffusivity, and mobility}
For the finite-$q$ TG measurements, the slow-component rates are regressed by unweighted least squares against $q^2$ as motivated by the diffusion equation. The slope is $(3.71\pm0.70)\times10^{-15}\,\si{\metre\squared\per\pico\second}$ ($R^2=0.934$; diagnostic uncertainty not estimated), giving an effective ambipolar diffusivity inferred from the slow TG component, $D_a=(3.71\pm0.70)\times10^{-3}\,\si{\metre\squared\per\second}$, where the stated error is the standard error of the four-point regression slope. Resampling complete retained scans gives a 95\% bootstrap interval $[2.31,4.96]\times10^{-3}\,\si{\metre\squared\per\second}$. This interval includes repeat-scan variation but not calibration or model uncertainty. Retaining borderline scan 4 while still excluding scan 10 gives $(3.78\pm0.56)\times10^{-3}\,\si{\metre\squared\per\second}$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:rate-q2}).

The present data thus permit an effective diffusion estimate under $1/\tau_{\rm TG}=1/\tau_{\rm rec}+D_aq^2$, provided the slow fitted component represents the same population across grating periods. They do not establish a unique material diffusivity: the PP rate disagrees with the TG extrapolation, and the fast and slow TG branches yield different slopes (Sec.~\ref{sec:chen-model}). We therefore interpret the value above specifically as the effective diffusivity of the slow fitted TG component. Model and grating-calibration uncertainties remain unquantified beyond the stated statistical errors.

\subsubsection{Additional model checks motivated by Chen et al.}\label{sec:chen-model}
Section IV of Chen et al.~\cite{chen} derives separate signals for grating imaging, total heterodyne detection and direct diffraction. Their weak-excitation total heterodyne expression, Eq.~(4), has the structure
\begin{equation}\label{eq:chen-total}
H(t)\propto F(t)\left[1+\frac12 e^{-D_aq^2t}\right],\qquad
F(t)=A_r e^{-t/\tau_r}+A_c e^{-t/\tau_c}.
\end{equation}
The non-diffusing term arises from the population response present in the total detected signal. The paper uses a GaAs/AlAs superlattice and a specific transmission geometry; neither its numerical lifetimes nor its fixed mixing coefficient can be transferred directly to our reflection experiment.

Under the tutorial's interpretation of the supplied TG traces as phase-extracted grating amplitudes, the appropriate quantity is the phase-dependent coefficient, rather than the whole signal in Eq.~\eqref{eq:chen-total}. This distinction follows explicitly by writing
\begin{align}
S(t,\psi)-B&=P(t)+G(t)\cos\psi,\\
\frac{S(t,\psi)+S(t,\psi+\pi)}{2}-B&=P(t),\\
\frac{S(t,\psi)-S(t,\psi+\pi)}{2\cos\psi}&=G(t)\quad(\cos\psi\ne0).
\end{align}
The last equation isolates the grating response; it removes the additive population term. The raw phase scans and extraction metadata are unavailable, so this channel interpretation remains an experimental assumption. Adding the paper's non-diffusing term to already extracted TG amplitudes without checking their definition would mix distinct observables.

For a common diffusivity acting on the two components, the isolated response would be
\begin{align}
G(t)&=F(t)e^{-D_aq^2t}\\
&=A_r e^{-(1/\tau_r+D_aq^2)t}+A_c e^{-(1/\tau_c+D_aq^2)t},\\
k_r(q)&=1/\tau_r+D_aq^2,\qquad
k_c(q)=1/\tau_c+D_aq^2.
\end{align}
Both rate branches must therefore have the same slope against $q^2$. Applying the existing analysis to both fitted branches gives
\begin{align}
D_{\rm fast}^{\rm eff}&=(2.05\pm0.63)\times10^{-2}\ \mathrm{m^2/s},\\
D_{\rm slow}^{\rm eff}&=(3.71\pm0.70)\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{m^2/s},
\end{align}
where the uncertainties are regression standard errors. These are diagnostic slopes, not two independently established material diffusivities. A paired 300-replicate bootstrap resamples whole retained scans and refits both components together, preserving their correlation. Its conditional 95\% interval for $D_{\rm fast}^{\rm eff}-D_{\rm slow}^{\rm eff}$ is $[0.72,2.72]\times10^{-2}$ m$^2$/s. Thus the simple common-diffusivity assignment is inconsistent with the independently fitted components within the retained-scan variability. Model, fit-window, channel-definition and grating-period uncertainties are excluded. Figure~\ref{fig:paper-model-diagnostics} shows the two rate branches. The slow-branch result remains an exploratory effective estimate rather than a validated common transport coefficient.

The paper also emphasizes measuring the population relaxation before constraining grating transport. Our PP residuals show that the existing biexponential-plus-constant model is inadequate over the complete record. As an empirical diagnostic, we therefore fit
\begin{equation}
S_{\rm PP}^{(3)}(t)=B+\sum_{j=1}^3 A_j e^{-t/\tau_j},\qquad
0<\tau_1<\tau_2<\tau_3,
\end{equation}
using the same unweighted fit window and retained repeats. The resulting times are $(0.674\pm0.011)$ ps, $(5.22\pm0.17)$ ps and $(619\pm60)$ ps, with conditional covariance standard errors. The normalized residual RMS decreases from 1.26\% to 0.303\%; uncertainties on these descriptive RMS diagnostics have not been estimated. The extra slow term captures much of the monotonic residual structure, although structured residuals remain (Fig.~\ref{fig:paper-model-diagnostics}). This does not prove a third carrier process: a non-exponential response or an evolving instrumental background can also produce a slow contribution. In particular, the fitted longest time is not established as a recombination lifetime. The change in the fast fitted time further demonstrates the model dependence of the cooling proxy. The original fit is retained as the stated baseline analysis, with this alternative explicitly shown as a model-sensitivity check. The calculations are reproducible with \texttt{analysis/paper\_model\_comparison.py}.

\section{Discussion}
The revised autocorrelation analysis gives pulses longer than the spectral Gaussian transform limit, with a shorter fitted duration after compression. Correcting the unequal overlap widths and the nonuniform fringe spacing removes the earlier sub-transform-limit estimates. The displayed envelope is a visualization of the fitted modulation bound; pulse deconvolution uses the raw-signal fit parameter. Residual structure and fit-window dependence remain, so the numerical durations are effective Gaussian-model estimates. An independently measured mirror trajectory and a phase-sensitive pulse measurement would help separate scan nonlinearity, pulse chirp, and detector response.

The fast PP lifetime can be quoted as a conditional cooling proxy, but the data do not establish the required proportionality between reflectivity and carrier temperature. Carrier thermalization, phonon scattering, energy cooling, and recombination are distinct processes. The cutoff at \SI{0.5}{ps} limits the earliest dynamics; the stronger reason for caution is the missing response calibration and the observed dependence on fit interval.

The main transport result is the finite-$q$ TG slope shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:rate-q2}. The fit yields
\begin{equation*}
D_a=(3.71\pm0.70)\times10^{-3}\,\si{\metre\squared\per\second}
\quad = (37.1\pm7.0)\,\si{\centi\metre\squared\per\second}.
\end{equation*}
The repeat-scan bootstrap interval is $[2.31,4.96]\times10^{-3}\,\si{\metre\squared\per\second}$. A bulk-GaAs pump--probe study reports an ambipolar diffusivity near $20\,\si{\centi\metre\squared\per\second}$ at room temperature (approximate literature value; uncertainty not specified in the cited abstract), so the present estimate is of the same order \cite{ruzicka}. This is not a controlled like-for-like comparison because our carrier density, fluence and measured sample temperature are unavailable; density-dependent effects can also change GaAs diffusivity \cite{young}.

The finite-$q$ regression intercept corresponds to a nominal recombination time of $(16.3\pm2.5)$ ps (first-order propagation of the regression intercept standard error). The pump--probe slow lifetime is \SI{7.878}{ps} with a conditional 95\% repeat-scan interval $[7.753,8.040]$ ps. Equivalently, its rate is $0.1269$ ps$^{-1}$ with a 95\% interval $[0.1244,0.1290]$ ps$^{-1}$, compared with the TG intercept $(0.0612\pm0.0095)$ ps$^{-1}$ (regression standard error); including the $q=0$ point reduces the linear-fit $R^2$ to 0.161 (diagnostic uncertainty not estimated). This disagreement is the central limitation of the pump--probe comparison: the $q=0$ reflectivity signal and finite-$q$ diffracted signal may weight different relaxation channels, while the simple rate law assumes one common population and one recombination intercept. Thus the TG-only slope is an exploratory estimate, and the intercept should not be reported as a measured recombination lifetime.

Using $\mu_a=eD_a/(k_BT)$ at an assumed $T=\SI{300}{K}$ gives a conditional mobility of $(1.44\pm0.27)\times10^3\,\si{\centi\metre\squared\per\volt\per\second}$. The mobility uncertainty propagates only the regression slope error at fixed assumed temperature; the corresponding 95\% repeat-scan interval is $[0.89,1.92]\times10^3$ cm$^2$/(V\,s). The nominal condition is room temperature, but the actual sample temperature and its uncertainty were not logged, and the Einstein conversion assumes non-degenerate carriers in thermal equilibrium at a common temperature. Therefore this mobility is conditional on assumptions rather than a direct measurement. Across the analysis, the main unresolved uncertainties are the delay and grating-period calibrations, the fit interval and biexponential model, limited grating-period sampling, scan-selection sensitivity, and missing acquisition metadata.

\section{Conclusion}
The reviewed analysis gives Gaussian pulse-intensity durations of $(112.89\pm0.10)$ fs before and $(93.902\pm0.054)$ fs after compression, compared with a $(83.87\pm0.24)$ fs transform-limit estimate from the spectrum. The quoted pulse and transform-limit errors are formal standard errors; total calibration and model uncertainties remain unquantified. Correct overlap widths and nonlinear fringe calibration are essential to these results. Fit-window effects exceed formal pulse-fit errors. The transient data yield reproducible effective decay constants for a specified window, but do not uniquely identify microscopic cooling or recombination. An effective cooling-time estimate of \SI{0.717}{ps} (95\% repeat-scan interval $[0.698,0.743]$ ps) can be reported under the assumption of a temperature-sensitive optical response, with additional model and assignment uncertainty unquantified; a volumetric electron--phonon coefficient cannot be obtained without additional heat-capacity and excitation information. The finite-$q$ TG slope gives an effective slow-component diffusivity $D_a=(3.71\pm0.70)\times10^{-3}$ m$^2$/s, with conditional mobility $(1.44\pm0.27)\times10^3$ cm$^2$/(V\,s) at an assumed \SI{300}{K} (temperature uncertainty unavailable). The diffusion and mobility errors are regression standard errors and exclude calibration uncertainty. The PP/TG intercept mismatch and measured fit-window sensitivity prevent a firm common recombination-lifetime assignment. The additional checks motivated by Chen et al. expose a slow PP contribution and unequal fast/slow rate slopes, further limiting the common-population transport interpretation (Sec.~\ref{sec:chen-model}).
