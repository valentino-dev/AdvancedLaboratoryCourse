\begin{abstract}
In this experiment, the relaxation and diffusion of photoexcited carriers in GaAs are studied using pump--probe and transient-grating measurements. First, the laser pulses are characterized by their spectrum and interferometric autocorrelation. The fitted pulse durations decrease from $(112.89\pm0.10)\,\si{fs}$ before compression to $(93.902\pm0.054)\,\si{fs}$ afterwards, while the spectrum gives a Gaussian transform limit of $(83.87\pm0.24)\,\si{fs}$. These uncertainties are formal standard errors; the dependence on the fitting interval is larger. We then fit the carrier signals with two exponential decays. From the dependence of the slow transient-grating rate on the grating period, we obtain an effective diffusion coefficient of $(3.71\pm0.70)\times10^{-3}\,\si{\metre\squared\per\second}$, with the uncertainty given by the regression standard error. However, the pump--probe decay does not agree with the extrapolation of these rates. The fitted diffusion coefficient therefore depends on the assumed assignment of the slow component, and a common recombination lifetime cannot be established. The available measurements also do not determine an electron--phonon coupling coefficient.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
When a semiconductor absorbs a short laser pulse, electrons are excited and leave holes behind. The excited carriers lose energy, recombine, and move through the sample. These processes change its optical properties, so a second, delayed pulse can be used to follow the response. In a pump--probe (PP) measurement, we measure the change in reflected probe light. In transient-grating (TG) spectroscopy, two pump beams instead create a periodic excitation pattern. Carriers diffuse from regions of high density into regions of lower density, reducing the contrast of this pattern. Measuring how quickly gratings of different periods disappear allows us to study carrier transport.

In this experiment, we study these processes in GaAs. Before analyzing the carrier dynamics, we determine the pulse duration and spectral bandwidth to characterize the excitation. The analysis then follows the four parts of the tutorial~\cite{tutorial}: pulse characterization, hot-carrier cooling, transient-reflectivity and grating decay times, and the determination of transport quantities. We compare the results with the expected diffusion dependence and discuss where the measurements are insufficient to assign a fitted decay to a particular physical process.

\section{Theoretical background}
\subsection{Transient-grating formation and carrier decay}\label{sec:decay-theory}
An ultrashort pump pulse creates electron--hole pairs in GaAs. Two pump fields with equal intensity $I_0$ and wave vectors $\mathbf{k}_1,\mathbf{k}_2$ produce the intensity pattern
\begin{align}
I(\mathbf{r})&=|E_1+E_2|^2\\
&=|E_1|^2+|E_2|^2+E_1E_2^*+E_1^*E_2\\
&=2I_0\left[1+\cos\left((\mathbf{k}_1-\mathbf{k}_2)\cdot\mathbf{r}+\phi\right)\right].
\end{align}
The distance between neighboring excitation maxima is the grating period $\Lambda_{\rm TG}$. We describe this spatial variation by the wave number $q=2\pi/\Lambda_{\rm TG}$. A smaller period therefore gives a larger $q$. At the same modulation amplitude, it also gives a steeper density gradient, so diffusion reduces the grating more quickly. The wave number describes variation in space, while the optical frequency describes oscillation in time. For an angle $\theta$ between the pump beams, $q=|\mathbf{k}_1-\mathbf{k}_2|=2k\sin(\theta/2)$, giving
\begin{equation}
\Lambda_{\rm TG}=\frac{2\pi}{q}=\frac{\lambda}{2\sin(\theta/2)}.
\end{equation}
The two-beam interference and resulting spatial carrier modulation are illustrated in Fig.~\ref{fig:tutorial-grating}.

\begin{figure}[!htbp]
\centering\includegraphics[width=.78\linewidth]{figures/tutorial_grating.png}
\caption{Two crossed pump beams create a sinusoidal excitation pattern in the sample. The grating period $\Lambda_{\rm TG}$ sets the spatial wave number $q=2\pi/\Lambda_{\rm TG}$ used in the diffusion analysis. Reproduced from Fig.~4.5 of the course tutorial.}\label{fig:tutorial-grating}
\end{figure}

Write the carrier density as a uniform part plus its sinusoidal grating component,
\begin{equation}
n(x,t)=N_0(t)+N_q(t)\cos(qx).
\end{equation}
Substitution into the tutorial's one-dimensional diffusion--recombination equation gives
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial n(x,t)}{\partial t}=D_a\frac{\partial^2 n(x,t)}{\partial x^2}-\frac{n(x,t)}{\tau_{\rm rec}}.
\end{equation}
Using $\partial_t n=\dot N_0+\dot N_q\cos(qx)$ and $\partial_x^2n=-q^2N_q\cos(qx)$, and equating the coefficients of $1$ and $\cos(qx)$, respectively,
\begin{align}
\dot N_0&=-\frac{N_0}{\tau_{\rm rec}}, & \dot N_q&=-\left(D_aq^2+\frac{1}{\tau_{\rm rec}}\right)N_q.\\
\int_{N_0(0)}^{N_0(t)}\frac{dN_0}{N_0}&=-\int_0^t\frac{dt'}{\tau_{\rm rec}}, &
\int_{N_q(0)}^{N_q(t)}\frac{dN_q}{N_q}&=-\int_0^t\left(D_aq^2+\frac{1}{\tau_{\rm rec}}\right)dt'.
\end{align}
Evaluation yields
\begin{align}
N_0(t)&=N_0(0)e^{-t/\tau_{\rm rec}},\\
N_q(t)&=N_q(0)e^{-(D_aq^2+1/\tau_{\rm rec})t}.
\end{align}
For heterodyne detection with a constant local oscillator and $E_{\rm dif}=\alpha N_q$,
\begin{align}
|E_{\rm LO}+E_{\rm dif}|^2&=|E_{\rm LO}|^2+|\alpha|^2N_q^2
 +2\operatorname{Re}(E_{\rm LO}^*\alpha)N_q,\\
S_{\rm het}&\propto N_q\propto e^{-(D_aq^2+1/\tau_{\rm rec})t}.
\end{align}
Thus the heterodyne decay rate contains $D_aq^2$, with no factor of two from squaring the grating amplitude. Assuming that the reflectivity change is also linear in the corresponding population, the $q=0$ transient-reflectivity and finite-$q$ grating traces therefore have the forms
\begin{align}
 S_{\mathrm{pop}}(t)&=A_{\mathrm{pop}}e^{-t/\tau_{\mathrm{rec}}}+C_{\mathrm{pop}},\\
 S_{\mathrm{TG}}(t)&=A_{\mathrm{TG}}e^{-t/\tau_{\mathrm{TG}}}+C_{\mathrm{TG}},\\
 \frac{1}{\tau_{\mathrm{TG}}}&=\frac{1}{\tau_{\mathrm{rec}}}+D_aq^2.
\end{align}
In $1/\tau_{\rm TG}=1/\tau_{\rm rec}+D_aq^2$, the intercept is $1/\tau_{\rm rec}$ and the slope is $D_a$.

The measured signals contain both a rapid initial decrease and a slower decay. Following the measurement instructions, we describe them by two exponentials and a constant background,
\begin{equation}
S(t,q)=C+A_f e^{-t/\tau_f}+A_s e^{-t/\tau_s},\qquad 0<\tau_f<\tau_s.
\end{equation}
We use the slow rate $1/\tau_s$ to examine the dependence on $q^2$. The diffusion equation predicts a straight line if this component follows the same carrier population at every grating period. It does not, however, derive the two-exponential form. The two fitted times initially describe the shape of the measured curves; their physical interpretation must be checked against the results.

\subsection{Ambipolar transport}
For ambipolar transport, set the excess electron and hole densities equal, $n=h$, and equate their particle fluxes. With the drift--diffusion expressions
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{j}_e=-D_e\nabla n-\mu_en\mathbf{E},\qquad
\mathbf{j}_h=-D_h\nabla n+\mu_hn\mathbf{E},
\end{equation}
the condition $\mathbf{j}_e=\mathbf{j}_h$ gives
\begin{align}
-(D_e-D_h)\nabla n&=(\mu_e+\mu_h)n\mathbf{E},\\
\mathbf{E}&=\frac{D_h-D_e}{\mu_e+\mu_h}\frac{\nabla n}{n},\\
\mathbf{j}_e&=-\left[D_e+\mu_e\frac{D_h-D_e}{\mu_e+\mu_h}\right]\nabla n\\
&=-\frac{D_e\mu_h+D_h\mu_e}{\mu_e+\mu_h}\nabla n.
\end{align}
Comparing with Fick's law $\mathbf{j}=-D_a\nabla n$ identifies
\begin{align}
D_a&=\frac{D_e\mu_h+D_h\mu_e}{\mu_e+\mu_h},\\
D_i&=\frac{k_BT}{e}\mu_i\quad(i=e,h)\\
\Longrightarrow\quad D_a&=\frac{2\mu_e\mu_h}{\mu_e+\mu_h}\frac{k_BT}{e}=\mu_a\frac{k_BT}{e},\\
\mu_a&=\frac{2\mu_e\mu_h}{\mu_e+\mu_h}.
\end{align}
The Einstein step assumes non-degenerate carriers at a common temperature. At high carrier density, many-body and degeneracy effects can change $D_a$ \cite{young}.

\subsection{Pulse duration and spectral bandwidth}
An autocorrelator splits each incoming laser pulse into two \emph{pulse replicas}: copies that travel through separate interferometer arms and are then recombined. Their \emph{optical delay} $\tau$ is the difference between their arrival times at the nonlinear crystal. For total propagation distances $L_1$ and $L_2$ in air, neglecting dispersion, $\tau\simeq(L_2-L_1)/c$. Equal travel times give $\tau=0$ and maximum temporal overlap. Moving one Michelson mirror by $\Delta z$ changes the round-trip path length by $2\Delta z$, so the delay changes by $\Delta\tau=2\Delta z/c$ (with the sign set by the chosen arm). Scanning this delay measures how the overlap changes, from which the pulse duration can be inferred under an assumed pulse shape.

For a Gaussian pulse intensity $I(t)=I_0e^{-t^2/(2\sigma_t^2)}$, the intensity autocorrelation is
\begin{align}
G(\tau)&=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}I(t)I(t-\tau)\,dt\\
&=I_0^2\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\exp\!\left[-\frac{t^2+(t-\tau)^2}{2\sigma_t^2}\right]dt\\
&=I_0^2e^{-\tau^2/(4\sigma_t^2)}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-(t-\tau/2)^2/\sigma_t^2}\,dt\\
&=I_0^2\sqrt{\pi}\sigma_t e^{-\tau^2/(4\sigma_t^2)}.
\end{align}
Here $t^2+(t-\tau)^2=2(t-\tau/2)^2+\tau^2/2$ and the substitution $u=(t-\tau/2)/\sigma_t$ reduces the integral to $\sigma_t\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-u^2}du=\sqrt{\pi}\sigma_t$. The intensity half-maximum satisfies $e^{-t^2/(2\sigma_t^2)}=1/2$, so $\Delta t_{\rm pulse}=2\sqrt{2\ln2}\,\sigma_t$. The autocorrelation half-maximum satisfies $e^{-\tau^2/(4\sigma_t^2)}=1/2$, so $\Delta t_{\rm AC}=4\sqrt{\ln2}\,\sigma_t=\sqrt{2}\,\Delta t_{\rm pulse}$.

The Gaussian time--bandwidth product follows from the field envelope $a(t)=\sqrt{I_0}e^{-t^2/(4\sigma_t^2)}$. Set $F(\nu)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}a(t)e^{-i2\pi\nu t}\,dt$ and use $a'(t)=-t\,a(t)/(2\sigma_t^2)$. Differentiating and integrating by parts on the real axis gives
\begin{align}
F'(\nu)&=-i2\pi\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}t\,a(t)e^{-i2\pi\nu t}\,dt\\
&=i4\pi\sigma_t^2\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}a'(t)e^{-i2\pi\nu t}\,dt\\
&=i4\pi\sigma_t^2\left([a(t)e^{-i2\pi\nu t}]_{-\infty}^{\infty}+i2\pi\nu F(\nu)\right)\\
&=-8\pi^2\sigma_t^2\nu F(\nu),\\
\int_{F(0)}^{F(\nu)}\frac{dF}{F}&=-8\pi^2\sigma_t^2\int_0^\nu\nu'\,d\nu',\\
F(\nu)&=F(0)e^{-4\pi^2\sigma_t^2\nu^2},\qquad
F(0)=2\sqrt{\pi}\sigma_t\sqrt{I_0},\\
|F(\nu)|^2&=4\pi\sigma_t^2I_0e^{-8\pi^2\sigma_t^2\nu^2}.
\end{align}
The boundary term vanishes because the Gaussian tends to zero at both limits; $F(0)$ follows by substituting $t=2\sigma_t u$ in the Gaussian integral.
At half maximum, $|F(\nu)|^2=|F(0)|^2/2$, so
\begin{align}
8\pi^2\sigma_t^2\nu^2&=\ln2,\\
\Delta\nu&=2|\nu|=\frac{\sqrt{\ln2}}{\sqrt{2}\pi\sigma_t}.
\end{align}
Combining this bandwidth with $\Delta t_{\rm pulse}=2\sqrt{2\ln2}\,\sigma_t$ gives
\begin{align}
\Delta t_{\rm pulse}\Delta\nu
&=2\sqrt{2\ln2}\,\sigma_t\frac{\sqrt{\ln2}}{\sqrt{2}\pi\sigma_t}\\
&=\frac{2\ln2}{\pi}\simeq0.441.
\end{align}
From $\nu=c/\lambda$, differentiation gives $d\nu/d\lambda=-c/\lambda^2$; for a narrow spectrum centered at $\lambda_0$, $\Delta\nu\simeq(c/\lambda_0^2)\Delta\lambda$.

These relations allow us to compare the duration measured by autocorrelation with the shortest Gaussian pulse supported by the spectrum. A larger time--bandwidth product can arise from chirp, although errors in the pulse model or delay conversion can also contribute. The comparison assumes that the fitted spectrum is representative of the pulse and approximately Gaussian. The interferometric signal measured in this experiment contains additional oscillating terms, which we derive below.

\section{Experimental setup and measurement}
\subsection{Optical arrangement}
The optical arrangement is shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:tutorial-setup}. According to the tutorial, the pulses are produced by a Kerr-lens mode-locked Ti:sapphire laser. Self-focusing favors pulsed operation, and a prism compressor compensates dispersion accumulated along the optical path. To measure the pulse duration, a Michelson interferometer splits the pulse into two replicas. Their delay is varied by moving one mirror, and their overlap is detected through second-harmonic generation in BBO, as shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:tutorial-autocorrelator}.

For the carrier measurements, the light is divided into pump and probe paths. The two pump beams cross on the GaAs sample and produce the excitation grating. In the BOXCAR arrangement, the diffracted probe interferes with a local oscillator, giving a signal linear in the weak diffracted field. Figure~\ref{fig:tutorial-heterodyne} shows this detection principle and its phase dependence. A rotating coverslip varies the local-oscillator phase, while a chopper modulates the pump. The lock-in amplifier extracts the response at the chopper frequency.

\subsection{Measurement data and calibration}
The measurements used here consist of the spectrum in \texttt{spektrum.csv}, three autocorrelation traces before compression (\texttt{vor1}--\texttt{vor3}), and three afterwards (\texttt{nach1}--\texttt{nach3}). There is also one PP dataset and one TG dataset for each of four grating periods. In these transient files, the first column contains the delay in picoseconds and the remaining columns contain repeated scans.

We take the supplied PP/TG delays and grating spacings as accurate and use them as exact inputs. The numbers 5.6, 6.5, 8, and 12 in the TG filenames give the phase-mask periods in micrometres. According to the accompanying email, the optical system reduces these periods by a factor of four at the sample. The sample period and wave number are therefore
\[
\Lambda_{\rm sample}=\frac{\Lambda_{\rm mask}}{4},
\qquad q=\frac{2\pi}{\Lambda_{\rm sample}}
=\frac{8\pi}{\Lambda_{\rm mask}}.
\]
For example, the \SI{12}{\micro\metre} mask gives a \SI{3}{\micro\metre} grating on the sample and $q=2.094\times10^6\,\si{\per\metre}$, rounded for display. Table~\ref{tab:tgfits} lists all four conversions. Since the periods and demagnification are taken as exact, no additional uncertainty is assigned to $q$ and the incidence angles are not needed for this calculation.

The horizontal coordinates in Fig.~\ref{fig:rate-q2} thus come from the grating geometry. To obtain the vertical coordinates, we average the retained scans, fit their decay, and calculate the slow rate as $k_s=1/\tau_s$. The PP measurement has no imposed periodic grating and is placed at $q=0$. Treating it as a uniform-population reference also assumes that diffusion out of the finite probe region can be neglected.

Figure~\ref{fig:vor-nach} compares the autocorrelation records. The sampling interval and total duration of \texttt{vor2} are five times shorter than those of the other records. Its oscillations also remain visible at the end of the measurement, so the signal is not fully covered. We quantify this truncation in Part I. For the remaining traces, we compare the residual root-mean-square (RMS), divided by the signal range in the fitted interval, and display the best-fitting trace from each group. This selects the smallest relative residual, rather than proving which trace has the smallest physical uncertainty.

\subsection{Data screening and fit procedure}
Before averaging, we compare the repeated scans to identify measurements that differ strongly from the rest. For TG datasets with at least eight repeats, each scan is compared with the pointwise median. We divide its deviations by the larger of the robust scale $1.4826\,\mathrm{MAD}$ and the lower quartile of that scale, then calculate their RMS. Here MAD denotes the median absolute deviation. From the resulting scores $s_j$, we calculate $z_j=0.6745(s_j-\mathrm{median}(s))/\mathrm{MAD}(s)$ and flag scans with $z_j>3.5$. The full flagged scans are excluded; individual delay points are retained. Figure~\ref{fig:excluded-scan-comparison} compares the excluded scans with the others. Since a statistical deviation does not prove an acquisition fault, we also repeat the diffusion analysis with these scans included.

We average the retained scans at each delay and calculate the standard error of the mean. The averaged curves are then fitted with the two-exponential function in Sec.~\ref{sec:decay-theory}, from \SI{0.5}{ps} to the final recorded delay. We use unweighted least squares, so every recorded point has equal weight even where the delay spacing changes. The error bars show the repeat-scan standard errors. The PP record extends to \SI{2000}{ps}, while the TG records end between \SI{26.6}{ps} and \SI{57}{ps}; we therefore also examine the effect of changing the fitting interval.

To estimate the uncertainty from repeat measurements, we use a bootstrap with 300 resamples (Appendix~\ref{app:bootstrap}). Each resample selects complete scans and repeats the averaging, fitting, and diffusion regression. The resulting 95\% repeat-scan intervals are explained in Appendix~\ref{app:bootstrap-intervals}. They describe repeat variability for the chosen model and scan selection, without including uncertainty in the model itself.

The files do not contain measured sample temperature, pump and probe fluence, spot size, sample thickness and orientation, incidence angles, or detector and lock-in settings. The tutorial and the \texttt{RT} filenames indicate room-temperature measurements, but no temperature reading is available. This missing information limits the comparison with literature. The Python analysis and numerical results are stored in \texttt{analysis/}.

\subsection{Uncertainty conventions and propagation}\label{sec:uncertainty}
We assess the fits using their residuals and, where repeat errors are available, the reduced chi-squared $\chi^2_\nu$. Appendix~\ref{app:chi-squared} gives the calculation and explains its limitations for these unweighted fits. For the spectrum and IAC, pointwise measurement errors are unknown, so a reduced chi-squared cannot be calculated independently.

Throughout the report, $\pm$ denotes one standard error unless stated otherwise. For nonlinear fits, it is calculated from the parameter covariance matrix; for straight-line fits, it is the ordinary least-squares regression error. The bracketed 95\% intervals for transient lifetimes and diffusivity instead come from the repeat-scan bootstrap. The two measures describe different sources of variation. Neither includes uncertainty in the physical interpretation, trace selection, or temperature. For the pulse measurements, wavelength calibration and the fitted delay conversion introduce further uncertainty that has not been quantified. The supplied PP/TG delays and grating spacings are treated as exact.

For a derived quantity $y=f(\mathbf p)$ with covariance matrix $\Sigma$, first-order propagation uses
\begin{equation}
u_y^2=\nabla f^{\mathsf T}\Sigma\nabla f.
\end{equation}
The full spectral covariance is retained for the peak level $A+B$, half-maximum level $A/2+B$, frequency bandwidth, and transform-limit duration. If $s$ is the fitted pulse width in carrier cycles, then $\Delta t\propto s\lambda_0$ and $\Delta\nu\propto\sigma_\lambda/\lambda_0^2$, so the time--bandwidth product is proportional to $s\sigma_\lambda/\lambda_0$. Its error propagation therefore includes the shared wavelength dependence rather than treating the derived duration and bandwidth as independent. Spectrum and IAC fit parameters from separate records are treated as independent. The common wavelength cancels in the before/after duration ratio. For the nominal intercept lifetime, $u_{1/b}=u_b/b^2$ is a first-order standard error, not a symmetric 95\% interval.

Theoretical constants, counts, file labels, selection thresholds, and chosen fitting intervals are specified inputs. We also identify descriptive quantities such as RMS and scan scores when their uncertainties have not been estimated. Treating the supplied transient delays as exact does not determine the autocorrelation delay: the oscilloscope timestamps must still be converted using the fringes, as derived in Sec.~\ref{sec:iac-derivation}. The error propagation is implemented in \texttt{analysis/report\_uncertainties.py}.

\section{Analysis and results}
\subsection{Part I: Pulse duration and spectral bandwidth}
We first compare the autocorrelation traces before and after compression in Fig.~\ref{fig:vor-nach}. The \texttt{vor2} signal is still oscillating strongly at the right boundary. To measure this, we calculate the RMS deviation from the whole-record median over the last 10\% of each record. The values are 0.397 recorded units for \texttt{vor2}, compared with 0.0081 and 0.0058 for \texttt{vor1} and \texttt{vor3}. Their uncertainties have not been estimated, and the median serves only as a reference level. This agrees with the visible truncation: \texttt{vor2} provides less information about the wings and background. We therefore exclude it from the pulse-duration calculation and use the more complete records.

\begin{figure}[!htbp]
\centering\includegraphics[width=.95\linewidth]{../plots/vor_nach.png}
\caption{Recorded auxiliary traces grouped as before (vor) and after (nach). The horizontal axis uses recorded time converted to milliseconds.}\label{fig:vor-nach}
\end{figure}


The measured spectrum and Gaussian fit are shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:spectrum}. The nominal wavelength coordinates (calibration uncertainty unavailable) span approximately \SI{345}{nm} to \SI{1041}{nm}. We fitted all recorded points with
\begin{equation}
S(\lambda)=B+A\exp\!\left[-\frac{(\lambda-\lambda_0)^2}{2\sigma_\lambda^2}\right].
\end{equation}
The peak above baseline is $A$ and the fitted peak level is $B+A$. At half peak height above baseline, $S-B=A/2$, so
\begin{align}
e^{-(\lambda-\lambda_0)^2/(2\sigma_\lambda^2)}&=\frac12,\\
|\lambda-\lambda_0|&=\sqrt{2\ln2}\,|\sigma_\lambda|,\\
\Delta\lambda_{\rm FWHM}&=2\sqrt{2\ln2}\,|\sigma_\lambda|.
\end{align}
The fit gives a centre of $(788.656\pm0.013)$ nm, peak intensity of $(13302\pm27)$ a.u., and fitted half-maximum level of $(7902\pm14)$ a.u., with $\Delta\lambda_{\rm FWHM}=(10.916\pm0.032)$ nm. Propagating the full spectral covariance through $\Delta\nu\simeq c\Delta\lambda/\lambda_0^2$ gives $\Delta\nu=(5.261\pm0.015)$ THz and $\Delta t_{\rm TL}=(2\ln2/\pi)/\Delta\nu=(83.87\pm0.24)$ fs. All quoted errors here are formal one-standard-error uncertainties, excluding calibration and model uncertainty. The median measured intensity is approximately \SI{2501}{a.u.}; its measurement uncertainty is unquantified because repeat spectra and an instrument-noise model are unavailable. The median is a descriptive statistic of all recorded wavelengths, not the fitted background $B$.

We then fit the autocorrelation records with Eq.~\eqref{eq:fitted-iac}. The resulting pulse durations are listed in Table~\ref{tab:iac-fits}. Of the traces before and after compression, \texttt{vor3} and \texttt{nach2} have the smallest normalized residuals and are shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:autocorrelation}. Their durations are $(112.89\pm0.10)\,\si{fs}$ and $(93.902\pm0.054)\,\si{fs}$, respectively. The corresponding time--bandwidth products are $0.5940\pm0.0018$ and $0.4941\pm0.0015$, both above the Gaussian limit $2\ln2/\pi$. The duration decreases by $(16.823\pm0.089)\%$, consistent with the action of the compressor. These results refer to the selected traces, rather than averages of the repeats. The errors follow from the fit covariance and include the shared wavelength dependence; pulse-model and wavelength-calibration uncertainties remain unquantified.

Figure~\ref{fig:fringe-calibration} in Appendix~\ref{sec:iac-residuals} shows why the delay conversion matters. The measured fringe maxima become closer together near the center of the scan, so equal intervals of recorded time cannot be treated as equal optical delays. Fitting the same intervals with a constant scan speed gives durations of $(74.53\pm0.39)$ fs and $(71.88\pm0.28)$ fs, with conditional standard errors. The normalized residual RMS values are then 0.107 and 0.080, compared with 0.0223 and 0.0134 for the cubic delay conversion; uncertainties on these diagnostics have not been estimated. The constant-speed assumption therefore describes the measured fringes poorly and underestimates the duration. We choose the nonlinear conversion from the fringe behavior and residuals.

Varying the fit half-window from \SI{2.0}{ms} to \SI{3.2}{ms} changes the fitted duration from $(111.65\pm0.08)$ fs to $(114.07\pm0.10)$ fs for \texttt{vor3}, and from $(92.69\pm0.05)$ fs to $(94.32\pm0.05)$ fs for \texttt{nach2} (conditional fit standard errors at fixed wavelength). The differences quantify sensitivity to the tested window choices; they are not confidence intervals for the pulse duration. The shifts exceed the conditional covariance errors of \SI{0.10}{fs} and \SI{0.05}{fs}. The errors assume independent residuals and an exact model; structured residuals, detector filtering, optical chirp, wavelength calibration, and trace selection are not included. Likewise, restricting the spectral fit to windows of $\pm50$, $\pm30$, and $\pm20$ nm about \SI{789}{nm} changes the FWHM to $(10.783\pm0.053)$, $(10.651\pm0.065)$, and $(10.434\pm0.078)$ nm, respectively, compared with $(10.916\pm0.032)$ nm for the full record (conditional fit standard errors). This background/window dependence exceeds the formal \SI{0.03}{nm} covariance error. These sensitivity ranges are diagnostics, not confidence intervals. The residuals are shown in Appendix~\ref{sec:iac-residuals}, Fig.~\ref{fig:iac-residuals}.

\subsection{Part II: Hot-carrier cooling and electron--phonon coupling}
The second part concerns the energy lost by photoexcited carriers to the lattice. Following the assignment in the measurement instructions, we examine the fast PP component as a possible cooling time. The fit described in Part III gives $\tau_{\rm cool}^{\rm eff}=\tau_f=\SI{0.717}{ps}$, with a 95\% repeat-scan bootstrap interval of $[0.698,0.743]$ ps. For this time to describe cooling, the fast reflectivity change must follow the excess carrier temperature. No calibration establishing this relation is available. The fit begins at \SI{0.5}{ps}, excluding the earliest thermalization but still covering picosecond cooling. The tutorial distinguishes roughly \SI{100}{fs} carrier thermalization from electron--lattice equilibration over a few picoseconds; uncertainties on these estimates are not supplied~\cite{tutorial}.

The fitted time also depends on how the PP response is described. Adding a third, slow exponential changes the fast time to $(0.674\pm0.011)$ ps, with a covariance standard error (Sec.~\ref{sec:chen-model}). This change and the systematic residuals show that repeat variability is only one source of uncertainty. We therefore use the fast component as an effective cooling-time estimate under the stated optical-response assumption. Its physical assignment is not included in the statistical interval.

To relate a cooling time to electron--phonon coupling, we assume constant volumetric heat capacities $C_e$ and $C_l$ for the electrons and lattice. With a coupling coefficient $G$, their energy exchange gives
\begin{align}
C_e\dot T_e&=-G(T_e-T_l), & C_l\dot T_l&=G(T_e-T_l),\\
\Delta T&=T_e-T_l, &
\frac{d\Delta T}{dt}&=-G\left(\frac1{C_e}+\frac1{C_l}\right)\Delta T,\\
\int_{\Delta T(0)}^{\Delta T(t)}\frac{d\Delta T'}{\Delta T'}
&=-G\left(\frac1{C_e}+\frac1{C_l}\right)\int_0^t dt',\\
\Delta T(t)&=\Delta T(0)e^{-t/\tau_{\rm cool}}, &
G&=\frac{C_eC_l}{C_e+C_l}\frac1{\tau_{\rm cool}}.
\end{align}
If $C_l\gg C_e$ and $\tau_{\rm cool}=\tau_f$, then $G/C_e\simeq1/\tau_f=\SI{1.394}{\per\pico\second}$, with a conditional bootstrap interval $[1.347,1.432]$ ps$^{-1}$. This is a relaxation rate under explicit assumptions, not a measured volumetric coupling coefficient in W\,m$^{-3}$\,K$^{-1}$. For nondegenerate electrons of fixed density $n$, $C_e=\partial(3nk_BT_e/2)/\partial T_e=3nk_B/2$, but $n$ and the relevant temperature dependence are unknown here. A numerical $G$ is therefore not determined.

The assignment also depends on the fit window: fitting PP only through \SI{33}{ps} gives $\tau_f=(0.579\pm0.010)$ ps, whereas starting the full-record fit at \SI{1}{ps} gives $(0.96\pm0.13)$ ps (conditional fit standard errors). These changes exceed the repeat-scan interval. For context, Hunsche et al. report thermalization within \SI{200}{fs}, while Kash et al. infer an average electron--LO-phonon scattering time near \SI{165}{fs} below $10^{17}$ cm$^{-3}$ \cite{hunsche,kash}. The quoted \SI{200}{fs} is a reported upper bound and \SI{165}{fs} is an approximate literature value; associated uncertainties are not supplied in the cited abstracts. Neither microscopic time is the same observable as the effective temperature-relaxation time or our reflectivity fit component.

\subsection{Part IV: Recombination, ambipolar diffusivity, and mobility}
We now use the fitted slow TG lifetimes to examine diffusion. For each grating period, we calculate $k_s=1/\tau_s$ and plot it against $q^2$ in Fig.~\ref{fig:rate-q2}. The diffusion model predicts a straight line. We describe its agreement with the data using the coefficient of determination $R^2$, explained in Appendix~\ref{app:r-squared}.

For the finite-$q$ TG measurements, the slow-component rates are regressed by unweighted least squares against $q^2$ as motivated by the diffusion equation. The slope is $(3.71\pm0.70)\times10^{-15}\,\si{\metre\squared\per\pico\second}$ ($R^2=0.934$; diagnostic uncertainty not estimated), giving an effective ambipolar diffusivity inferred from the slow TG component, $D_a=(3.71\pm0.70)\times10^{-3}\,\si{\metre\squared\per\second}$, where the stated error is the standard error of the four-point regression slope. Resampling complete retained scans gives a 95\% bootstrap interval $[2.31,4.96]\times10^{-3}\,\si{\metre\squared\per\second}$. This interval includes repeat-scan variation but not model uncertainty. \subsubsection{Comparison of population and grating decay models}\label{sec:chen-model}
Section IV of Chen et al.~\cite{chen} derives separate signals for grating imaging, total heterodyne detection and direct diffraction. Their weak-excitation total heterodyne expression, Eq.~(4), has the structure
\begin{equation}\label{eq:chen-total}
H(t)\propto F(t)\left[1+\frac12 e^{-D_aq^2t}\right],\qquad
F(t)=A_r e^{-t/\tau_r}+A_c e^{-t/\tau_c}.
\end{equation}
The non-diffusing term arises from the population response present in the total detected signal. The paper uses a GaAs/AlAs superlattice and a specific transmission geometry; neither its numerical lifetimes nor its fixed mixing coefficient can be transferred directly to our reflection experiment.

Under the tutorial's interpretation of the supplied TG traces as phase-extracted grating amplitudes, the appropriate quantity is the phase-dependent coefficient, rather than the whole signal in Eq.~\eqref{eq:chen-total}. This distinction follows explicitly by writing
\begin{align}
S(t,\psi)-B&=P(t)+G(t)\cos\psi,\\
\frac{S(t,\psi)+S(t,\psi+\pi)}{2}-B&=P(t),\\
\frac{S(t,\psi)-S(t,\psi+\pi)}{2\cos\psi}&=G(t)\quad(\cos\psi\ne0).
\end{align}
The last equation isolates the grating response; it removes the additive population term. The raw phase scans and extraction metadata are unavailable, so this channel interpretation remains an experimental assumption. Adding the paper's non-diffusing term to already extracted TG amplitudes without checking their definition would mix distinct observables.

For a common diffusivity acting on the two components, the isolated response would be
\begin{align}
G(t)&=F(t)e^{-D_aq^2t}\\
&=A_r e^{-(1/\tau_r+D_aq^2)t}+A_c e^{-(1/\tau_c+D_aq^2)t},\\
k_r(q)&=1/\tau_r+D_aq^2,\qquad
k_c(q)=1/\tau_c+D_aq^2.
\end{align}
Both rate branches must therefore have the same slope against $q^2$. Applying the existing analysis to both fitted branches gives
\begin{align}
D_{\rm fast}^{\rm eff}&=(2.05\pm0.63)\times10^{-2}\ \mathrm{m^2/s},\\
D_{\rm slow}^{\rm eff}&=(3.71\pm0.70)\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{m^2/s},
\end{align}
where the uncertainties are regression standard errors. These are diagnostic slopes, not two independently established material diffusivities. A paired 300-replicate bootstrap resamples whole retained scans and refits both components together, preserving their correlation. Its conditional 95\% interval for $D_{\rm fast}^{\rm eff}-D_{\rm slow}^{\rm eff}$ is $[0.72,2.72]\times10^{-2}$ m$^2$/s. Thus the simple common-diffusivity assignment is inconsistent with the independently fitted components within the retained-scan variability. Model, fit-window and channel-definition uncertainties are excluded; the supplied delays and grating spacings are treated as exact. Figure~\ref{fig:paper-model-diagnostics} shows the two rate branches. The slow-branch result remains an exploratory effective estimate rather than a validated common transport coefficient.

The population response must also be described adequately before it can be compared with grating transport. Since the PP residuals show a long-lived contribution, we repeat the fit with three exponentials and a constant,
\begin{equation}
S_{\rm PP}^{(3)}(t)=B+\sum_{j=1}^3 A_j e^{-t/\tau_j},\qquad
0<\tau_1<\tau_2<\tau_3,
\end{equation}
using the same interval and scans. This gives $(0.674\pm0.011)$ ps, $(5.22\pm0.17)$ ps, and $(619\pm60)$ ps, with conditional covariance standard errors. The normalized residual RMS falls from 1.26\% to 0.303\%; uncertainties on these diagnostics have not been estimated. As shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:paper-model-diagnostics}, the extra term accounts for much of the long-time trend, although structure remains. A slow instrumental background or a non-exponential response could also produce this improvement, so the longest fitted time cannot be assigned directly to recombination. The change in the fast time also affects its interpretation as cooling. We use the two-exponential fits for the main comparison and the three-exponential fit to show the dependence on the chosen model. This calculation is implemented in \texttt{analysis/paper\_model\_comparison.py}.

\section{Discussion}
The autocorrelation results show a shorter pulse after the compressor, as expected. Both fitted durations remain above the Gaussian transform limit obtained from the spectrum. In calculating these durations, the two overlap terms must be kept distinct and the changing fringe spacing must be included in the delay conversion. The remaining residual structure and dependence on the fitting interval show that the Gaussian fit does not fully describe the recorded signal. Measuring the mirror trajectory independently and using a phase-sensitive pulse measurement would help distinguish scan nonlinearity, chirp, and detector response.

For the carrier measurements, the main difficulty is assigning an optical decay time to a physical process. The fast PP component may describe cooling if the reflectivity change follows carrier temperature. This proportionality has not been measured, and the fitted time changes with the chosen interval and number of exponential terms. We can therefore compare its timescale with cooling, but cannot identify it uniquely with a temperature-relaxation time. Thermalization, individual phonon scattering events, cooling, and recombination describe different processes and need not have the same characteristic time.

The main transport result is the finite-$q$ TG slope shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:rate-q2}. The fit yields
\begin{equation*}
D_a=(3.71\pm0.70)\times10^{-3}\,\si{\metre\squared\per\second}
\quad = (37.1\pm7.0)\,\si{\centi\metre\squared\per\second}.
\end{equation*}
The repeat-scan bootstrap interval is $[2.31,4.96]\times10^{-3}\,\si{\metre\squared\per\second}$. A bulk-GaAs pump--probe study reports an ambipolar diffusivity near $20\,\si{\centi\metre\squared\per\second}$ at room temperature (approximate literature value; uncertainty not specified in the cited abstract), so the present estimate is of the same order \cite{ruzicka}. This is not a controlled like-for-like comparison because our carrier density, fluence and measured sample temperature are unavailable; density-dependent effects can also change GaAs diffusivity \cite{young}.

The finite-$q$ regression intercept corresponds to a nominal recombination time of $(16.3\pm2.5)$ ps (first-order propagation of the regression intercept standard error). The pump--probe slow lifetime is \SI{7.878}{ps} with a conditional 95\% repeat-scan interval $[7.753,8.040]$ ps. Equivalently, its rate is $0.1269$ ps$^{-1}$ with a 95\% interval $[0.1244,0.1290]$ ps$^{-1}$, compared with the TG intercept $(0.0612\pm0.0095)$ ps$^{-1}$ (regression standard error); including the $q=0$ point reduces the linear-fit $R^2$ to 0.161 (diagnostic uncertainty not estimated). This disagreement is the central limitation of the pump--probe comparison: the $q=0$ reflectivity signal and finite-$q$ diffracted signal may be sensitive to different mixtures of relaxation processes, while the simple rate law assumes one common population and one recombination intercept. Thus the TG-only slope is an exploratory estimate, and the intercept should not be reported as a measured recombination lifetime.

Here, relaxation processes are physical mechanisms through which the excited sample returns toward equilibrium. Carrier cooling transfers excess carrier energy to the lattice; recombination reduces the number of excited electron--hole pairs; and diffusion redistributes carriers from densely excited regions into less excited regions, reducing the TG modulation without itself removing carriers. These processes can all affect the measured optical response, with different relative contributions to PP reflectivity and TG diffraction. Consequently, the fitted slow PP and TG components may approximate different mixtures of processes. Calling both components ``slow'' does not identify them with the same recombination mechanism. The two exponential terms are phenomenological descriptions of the traces; fitting them does not establish exactly two independent microscopic relaxation processes or uniquely assign a physical mechanism to either fitted lifetime.

Using $\mu_a=eD_a/(k_BT)$ at an assumed $T=\SI{300}{K}$ gives a conditional mobility of $(1.44\pm0.27)\times10^3\,\si{\centi\metre\squared\per\volt\per\second}$. The mobility uncertainty propagates only the regression slope error at fixed assumed temperature; the corresponding 95\% repeat-scan interval is $[0.89,1.92]\times10^3$ cm$^2$/(V\,s). The nominal condition is room temperature, but the actual sample temperature and its uncertainty were not logged, and the Einstein conversion assumes non-degenerate carriers in thermal equilibrium at a common temperature. Therefore this mobility is conditional on assumptions rather than a direct measurement. For the carrier analysis, the main unresolved issues are the fit interval and biexponential model, limited sampling of grating periods, scan-selection sensitivity, and missing acquisition metadata. Delay and grating-spacing calibration are treated as exact inputs rather than possible explanations of the PP/TG mismatch.

\section{Conclusion}
In this experiment, we characterized the laser pulses and studied the decay of photoexcited carriers in GaAs. The fitted pulse duration decreased from $(112.89\pm0.10)$ fs before compression to $(93.902\pm0.054)$ fs afterwards. Both values are above the spectral Gaussian transform limit of $(83.87\pm0.24)$ fs. The quoted errors are formal standard errors, while changes with the fitting interval show a larger uncertainty associated with the pulse model.

The PP and TG signals were described by fast and slow exponential decays. The fast PP time of \SI{0.717}{ps}, with a 95\% repeat-scan interval of $[0.698,0.743]$ ps, gives an effective cooling-time estimate if the optical response follows carrier temperature. Without that calibration and the carrier heat capacity, an electron--phonon coupling coefficient cannot be determined.

The slow TG rates increase with $q^2$, giving an effective diffusivity of $(3.71\pm0.70)\times10^{-3}$ m$^2$/s. At an assumed \SI{300}{K}, this corresponds to a mobility of $(1.44\pm0.27)\times10^3$ cm$^2$/(V\,s). These errors follow from the regression at fixed supplied delays and grating spacings; model uncertainty and the unavailable temperature uncertainty are excluded. The PP rate does not agree with the TG intercept, and the independently fitted fast and slow TG components give different slopes. Thus the measurements support a dependence on grating spacing, but do not establish one common diffusion coefficient and recombination lifetime for all fitted components.
